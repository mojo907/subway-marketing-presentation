Subway has about 35,800 restaurants worldwide and is still the top sandwich chain by far. But in the US, its home market, system sales fell about 6% in 2025 to $8.9 billion, after a 3.8% fall in 2024. At the same time its closest rival, Jersey Mike's, grew 13%.

So our key question is: how can Subway become the automatic lunch choice again? We answer it in five steps: the macro environment with PESTLE, then SWOT, the 5C's, segmentation, targeting and positioning, and finally the consumer decision process, before one recommendation.

PESTLE

Subway's growth depends on how well it responds to rising costs and changing consumer expectations

DefinitionA PESTLE analysis is a strategic business framework used to identify, evaluate, and monitor the macro-environmental (external) factors that can affect an organization, industry, or project.

Presented by Chelsea

Definition. A PESTLE analysis is a strategic business framework used to identify, evaluate, and monitor the macro-environmental (external) factors that can affect an organization, industry, or project.

Political. Impact of international trade agreements: Trade agreements can reduce tariffs and make it easier for Subway to source ingredients and operate across international markets. Impact of geopolitical instability: Political conflicts and international tensions can disrupt supply chains, increase costs and affect Subway’s reputation. Its former Russian franchise operations also highlight the reputational risks of operating in politically sensitive markets.

Economic. Rising costs and inflation: Higher prices for ingredients, energy, transport and labour increase operating costs and put pressure on Subway’s profit margins. Changing customer perceptions of value: As the cost of living rises, customers become more price-conscious. Subway’s historic $5 Footlong promotion helped establish its value image, while concerns about smaller portions and higher prices may affect customer perceptions. Increasing competition and pressure on franchise profitability: Subway competes with other fast-food chains offering affordable meals and convenient services. Franchisees must balance competitive prices with rising operating costs, which can affect profitability and the customer experience.

Social. Growing demand for healthier food: Consumers are increasingly looking for healthier food options, creating opportunities for Subway to promote its salads, fresh ingredients and customisable sandwiches. However, customers may still be concerned about the nutritional content of some menu items. Increasing awareness of ingredients and food transparency: Consumers are more interested in where ingredients come from and whether food contains GMOs, additives or artificial ingredients. This increases pressure on Subway to communicate clearly about its ingredients and sourcing. Growing demand for convenience and digital experiences: Customers increasingly expect quick service, mobile ordering and home delivery. Popular culture, including product placement in Korean dramas, can also help increase brand awareness among younger audiences.

Technological. Growing use of digital technology in the fast-food industry: Mobile apps, online ordering, self-service kiosks and loyalty programmes can improve convenience, customer engagement and operational efficiency. Increasing reliance on third-party delivery platforms and cybersecurity: Delivery platforms help Subway reach more customers, but their commission fees can reduce profits. Collecting customer data through digital services also creates cybersecurity and data-protection responsibilities.

Legal. Increasing requirements for food labelling and nutritional information: Subway must follow rules on nutritional information, ingredient lists and allergen declarations. Changes in these regulations may require updates to menus, packaging and customer information. Strict GMO and food safety regulations: Different countries have different requirements for genetically modified ingredients and food safety. Subway must ensure that its ingredients, preparation methods and food-handling procedures meet local standards. Changes in employment laws and labour regulations: Minimum wage increases, working-hour restrictions and employee rights regulations can increase labour costs and affect how Subway manages its restaurants.

Environmental. Increasing impact of climate change on supply chains: Extreme weather and changing climate conditions can affect the availability and price of fresh produce, creating supply disruptions and higher costs. Growing pressure to reduce food waste and use sustainable packaging: Subway faces pressure to reduce food waste and use more sustainable packaging. Its large restaurant network also means that energy consumption, transport and waste management contribute to its environmental footprint.

Conclusion: Subway has ambitious goals to strengthen its market position and expand internationally. Achieving these goals will depend on how effectively it responds to external challenges, such as rising costs and changing consumer expectations. By maintaining strong franchise performance and customer appeal, Subway can support its long-term growth and expansion.

Likely questions

Which factor matters most for Subway today? Economic. Rising costs squeeze margins while customers become more price-conscious, so franchisees must balance competitive prices with rising operating costs.

Why does geopolitics matter for a sandwich chain? Conflicts can disrupt supply chains and raise costs, and operating in politically sensitive markets carries reputational risk, as Subway's former Russian franchise operations showed.

SWOT

Subway's brand and network are strong, but perception and consistency hold it back

HelpfulHarmful Internal External
Presented by Kira

About the SWOT. SWOT sorts factors on two axes: internal or external, and helpful or harmful. Strengths are internal advantages that help a company achieve its objectives. Weaknesses are internal limitations that put a company at a disadvantage. Opportunities are external trends or developments a company can capitalize on. Threats are external factors that could negatively affect a company’s performance.

Applied to Subway. Strengths: strong global brand recognition, its network of locations, advanced franchising, a flexible restaurant format and a healthy brand positioning. Weaknesses: brand perception, franchise inconsistency, customer experience, marketing and promotions, menu and sourcing, outdated facilities and product differentiation. Opportunities: the Blueprint initiative, digital ordering and delivery, growing interest in a personalized ordering format and international market growth. Threats: a declining US market presence, intense competition, changing consumer preferences and price-sensitive consumers. In short, the brand and the network are strong, but perception and consistency hold Subway back.

Likely questions

What is the Blueprint initiative? Subway's new EMEA restaurant format, rolling out from 2027. It is built around self-order kiosks and the app, and adds breakfast and coffee (from Naud's 5C's research).

Which weakness matters most? Brand perception and product differentiation: Subway is perceived as lower quality than newer sandwich rivals such as Jersey Mike's, which makes it harder to stand out on anything but price.

5C's

Subway has global scale, but must fix franchisee profitability before Jersey Mike's takes more share

DefinitionThe 5C's analyse a brand's situation: Company, Customers, Competitors, Collaborators and Context.

Presented by Naud

The 5C's give a full picture of the situation. Company: Subway is fully franchised, owned by Roark Capital since 2024, with about 35,800 units and $16 billion in global sales. But US system sales fell about 6% to $8.9 billion in 2025. Customers: value-seeking diners, office workers and students who want price, speed, customisation and a perception of freshness and protein. Competitors: Jersey Mike's is the rising threat, with $4.3 billion in sales, up 13%, and about $1.36 million per store. McDonald's and Taco Bell attack on value. Collaborators: US franchisees are the critical partner and a strained one, while master franchisees are the new growth engine with over 12,000 future units, including about 400 in Spain with Grupo Vierci. Context: a sector-wide value war. To conclude: Subway has scale and an international pipeline, but at home it is shrinking. The key question is whether it can make franchisees profitable again before Jersey Mike's takes more share.

Likely questions

Why are franchisees strained? Average store sales are around $500,000, with an 8% royalty and a 4.5% ad fund. Franchisees of over a quarter of stores petitioned against the Sub Club offer as too generous (revised April 1, 2026), and several operators filed for bankruptcy.

Why is Jersey Mike's the main threat if Subway is six times bigger by store count? The top 15 sandwich brands' sales were roughly flat in 2025 (Guggenheim), so its 13% growth comes at Subway's expense. It also listed on the NYSE in July 2026 at a $7.3 billion valuation.

Segmentation means dividing the market into groups of consumers with similar needs. Targeting means choosing which of those groups the brand serves first. The main consumer profile is the time-conscious consumer: on-the-go, value-oriented and choice-driven. They look for quick, accessible meals that fit their lifestyle, budget and personal preferences. Subway segments this market on five bases. Geographic: urban areas and busy city locations. Demographic: students and young professionals, across different income levels. Psychographic: busy lifestyles, convenience and personal choice. Benefits sought: speed, affordability and customisation. Behavioural: lunch occasions, quick-service usage and repeat purchases. That leads to the target market: time-pressed consumers who value convenience, affordability and customisation. In one persona: urban students and young professionals aged 18 to 34.

Likely questions

Which segmentation base matters most for Subway? Benefits sought. Speed, affordability and customisation are what separate Subway's customers from those of burger chains or premium sandwich shops.

Why this target and not families? Their needs match what Subway offers, and they live, study and work in the busy city locations where Subway stores are.

Consumer Decision Process Model

Subway wins the lunch decision when it is the fastest, cheapest and most flexible option

Order complete: Subway becomes the habit

Definitionthe 5 stages a consumer goes through before and after a purchase. For a fast-food lunch, it happens in minutes and is often driven by habit.

Presented by Joé

Thank you, Maëa. Now let's see how that customer actually decides. The Consumer Decision Process Model describes the five stages a consumer goes through before and after a purchase. For a fast-food lunch, it happens in minutes, and it is often driven by habit.

Definition. The Consumer Decision Process Model describes the five stages a consumer goes through before and after buying: (1) need recognition, (2) information search, (3) evaluation of alternatives, (4) purchase decision and (5) post-purchase behaviour. Marketers use it to understand where they can influence the customer at each step. For a low-involvement, habitual purchase like a fast-food lunch, the stages happen in minutes and the consumer often skips straight from need to purchase, which makes habit, availability and loyalty very important.

It starts with need recognition. You have thirty minutes between two classes. You are hungry, and you want something quick and a bit lighter.

1. Need recognition. Trigger: hunger at lunchtime, a short break between classes or meetings, or a craving for something “lighter” than burgers and fries. Subway example: a student in Barcelona has 30 minutes between classes and wants something quick, filling and not too expensive. How Subway activates the need: push notifications on the app, lunchtime social media posts, and visible stores in high-traffic areas (stations, campuses, malls).

Second, information search. You open Google Maps or a delivery app, and with its huge network, Subway is almost always on your screen.

2. Information search. Internal search: memory of past visits (“I know what I order at Subway”). External search: Google Maps (“food near me”), delivery apps (Uber Eats, Glovo, Just Eat), the Subway app, TikTok / Instagram and friends’ recommendations. How Subway influences it: large store footprint means it often appears first on maps, presence on all delivery marketplaces, and menu prices and deals visible in the app.

Third, evaluation of alternatives: the key stage. You compare price, speed, customisation and freshness against McDonald's, Jersey Mike's or the local bocadillo.

3. Evaluation of alternatives. Key criteria for our target (time-conscious students and young professionals): price, speed, customisation, perceived healthiness / freshness, and taste. Alternatives considered: McDonald’s and Burger King (cheap and fast), Jersey Mike’s (perceived higher quality), Chipotle or salad chains (healthier), and locally a bocadillo shop or supermarket ready meal. Subway’s advantage: “freshly made, never fried”, fully customisable and with protein-rich options. Its weakness: perceived lower quality than newer sandwich rivals and strong value deals from burger chains.

Fourth, the purchase decision. The Fresh Value Menu, with fifteen items under five dollars, and your Sub Club points push you to say yes.

4. Purchase decision. Where and how: in store at the counter (the customer builds the sandwich step by step), via the app or kiosk, or through delivery. What pushes the final choice: the Fresh Value Menu (15 items under $5, launched April 2026), Sub Club loyalty rewards, and the speed of the line at that moment. Risk factors at this stage: a long queue, a closed or empty store, or a better deal from a competitor can still change the decision at the last second.

And fifth, post-purchase behaviour. If you are satisfied, it becomes a habit. If you are disappointed, you regret it and may leave a bad review.

5. Post-purchase behaviour. Satisfaction: if the sandwich was fresh, fast and good value, the customer is likely to repeat and the purchase becomes a habit. Dissatisfaction / cognitive dissonance: if the bread is dry or the price felt high, the customer may think “I should have gone to Jersey Mike’s” and leave a negative review. How Subway manages it: Sub Club points to bring customers back, app feedback, and online reviews that feed the information search of the next customers. This closes the loop back to stage 1.

Order complete: Subway becomes the habit.

Wrap. Order complete: when the experience is fresh, fast and good value, Subway becomes the habit and the customer skips the comparison next time. Tip for Q&A: hover or click a layer to bring back the detail of that stage.

Likely questions

Why do you call this a low-involvement decision? A lunch is cheap, frequent and low risk, so the five stages happen in minutes and the customer often goes straight from need to purchase. That is why habit, availability and loyalty matter so much.

At which stage can Subway gain the most? Stage 3, evaluation. Subway is already visible in stages 1 and 2. It has to convince customers that fresh and customisable is worth choosing over cheaper burger deals and higher-quality sandwich rivals.

Consumer Decision Process: conclusion

The lunch battle is won or lost at stage 3, so Subway must become the automatic choice

1Strong in stages 1 and 2: huge store network and delivery presence = always visible
2Stage 3 is the battleground: cheaper burger deals vs higher-quality sandwich rivals
3Priority: strengthen value perception (Fresh Value Menu) and loyalty (Sub Club)
4Goal: make Subway the automatic choice so customers skip the comparison
Presented by Joé

So, what does this mean for Subway?

Subway is strong in stages one and two: with its huge network and delivery presence, it is always visible.

The battleground is stage three, between cheaper burger deals and higher-quality sandwich rivals.

So the priority is to strengthen value perception with the Fresh Value Menu, and loyalty with Sub Club.

The goal: make Subway the automatic choice, so customers skip the comparison. And that leads us to our overall recommendation.

For Subway, the lunch decision is fast, habitual and highly price-sensitive. Subway is strong in the first two stages thanks to its huge store network and delivery presence, so it is almost always visible when hunger hits. The battle is won or lost in stage 3 (evaluation), where Subway must convince customers that its fresh, customisable offer is worth choosing over cheaper burger deals and higher-quality sandwich rivals. The key marketing priority is therefore to strengthen value perception (Fresh Value Menu) and post-purchase loyalty (Sub Club), so that Subway becomes the automatic, repeated choice and customers skip the comparison altogether.

Likely questions

How does Sub Club help in this model? It works on stage 5: points bring customers back, repetition builds a habit, and a habit means the customer skips stages 2 and 3 next time.

Is more discounting not a problem for franchisees? Yes, it is a real tension. Franchisees of over a quarter of stores petitioned against the Sub Club offer as too generous and it was revised on April 1, 2026. Value has to be designed so that it stays profitable for them.

Our recommendation

Subway should turn its scale into a daily habit for value-seeking urban lunch customers

1PESTLE and 5C'sDefend value: keep the Fresh Value Menu visible in a market at war on price
2SWOTProve freshness: close the quality perception gap with newer sandwich rivals
3Targeting and positioningOwn the target: urban 18 to 34s who want fast, affordable and customisable
4Consumer decision processBuild the habit: Sub Club and the app make Subway the automatic choice

Let us connect everything. PESTLE and the 5C's show a market at war on price, so Subway has to defend value and keep the Fresh Value Menu visible. SWOT shows that the brand and the network are strengths but brand perception and product differentiation are weaknesses, so Subway has to prove freshness, not only claim it. Segmentation, targeting and positioning tell us who to win: urban students and young professionals, 18 to 34, who want fast, affordable and customisable meals. And the consumer decision process shows how: win the evaluation stage and then build the habit with Sub Club and the app. Our recommendation in one sentence: Subway should turn its scale into a daily habit for value-seeking urban lunch customers. More repeat visits mean higher sales per store, which is exactly what franchisees need.

Likely questions

How would you measure success? Sales per store (today about $500,000), repeat visits through Sub Club, and the share of orders through the app and delivery.

What is the main risk of this recommendation? Value offers that franchisees cannot afford. They already pushed back on discounting, so offers must protect their margins.
